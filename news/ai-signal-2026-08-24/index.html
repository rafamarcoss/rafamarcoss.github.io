AI in 2026: Labor Urgency, Education Under Strain, and the Era of Results

AI is consolidating as a professional standard, but training is failing and classroom learning is suffering. Governments and companies seek concrete results while controlling spending.

24 August 20264 min read

Artificial intelligence is no longer a promise: in 2026 it demands fluency, redefines professional diligence, and is applied to public problems like housing. However, the urgency to adopt it coexists with training errors, educational risks, and the need to justify every euro invested. This edition connects these tensions and offers practical keys to avoid falling behind.

Work and Training: The Urgency of Fluency Clashes with Deficient Programs

Jon Hernández, an AI expert, warns that within two years, anyone not fluent in artificial intelligence will be left out of the job market. That pressure translates into initiatives like Serveo's, which has deployed 733 AI agents created by its own employees, an example of automation driven from the ground up.

But training alone is not enough: Negocios.com identifies six common errors that hinder productivity, from generic content to lack of follow-up. Companies need programs that go beyond theory and connect with real tasks, or training spending will not translate into results.

Education: Between Reduced Learning and Necessary Literacy

A study cited by La Vanguardia concludes that using AI to do homework and assignments reduces learning by 20%. This data alerts us to the passive use of these tools, which can replace the cognitive effort needed to consolidate knowledge.

In parallel, Microsoft and San Jorge University launch an initiative to bring AI literacy into classrooms. The tension is clear: we must teach how to use AI without it canceling the learning process. Educational policies must integrate the tool with pedagogical design that preserves critical thinking.

Regulation and Professional Responsibility: AI as a Standard of Diligence

Lawyers consulted by El Español agree that a professional can incur negligence by not using artificial intelligence. This represents a profound change: AI is no longer optional and becomes an enforceable standard of diligence, with legal implications.

Business Insider España analyzes how AI transforms law firms, automating document review and outcome prediction. Firms that do not adopt these tools not only lose competitiveness but also assume a risk of professional liability. The boundary between support and substitution remains unclear, but the direction is irreversible.

Applied Technology and Results: Controlling Spending, Measuring Impact

Catalonia will use artificial intelligence to locate vacant apartments and bring them to market, an example of public application with direct social impact. Data analysis will identify properties and act on supply, although the news does not detail data protection mechanisms.

In the business sphere, Computer Weekly addresses how to regain control of AI spending, while MUNDIARIO announces the entry into the era of results: companies demand return metrics. The combination requires financial and technical governance: knowing how much is invested, where it is invested, and what is obtained.

What matters

  1. Review AI training programs and avoid the six common errors so that investment translates into productivity.
  2. In education, integrate AI with methodologies that avoid replacing cognitive effort, prioritizing critical literacy.
  3. For legal professionals, evaluate the adoption of AI tools as part of due diligence and risk management.
  4. Control AI spending through return metrics and financial governance, following Computer Weekly's guidelines and the era of results.
  5. Explore AI applications with social impact, such as detecting vacant housing, always with appropriate data frameworks.

AI is no longer a matter of the future: it demands decisions today. Fluency has become a condition of employability, professional diligence is being redefined, and education must balance use and learning. Those who integrate technology with clear strategy, effective training, and spending control will be better positioned. The rest, as Jon Hernández warns, risk being left behind.
