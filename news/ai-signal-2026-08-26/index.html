AI in Spain: Record Investment, Labor Gap, and Educational Dilemmas

Alibaba and SoftBank mobilize €14.1 billion as AI drives three out of ten business tasks. Legal and healthcare professionals demand training, and education seeks to redesign homework in the face of learning loss.

26 August 20265 min read

Artificial intelligence today moves between investor euphoria and adoption challenges. Major tech companies are betting billions on infrastructure and autonomous agents, but the labor and educational fabric shows tensions: employees adopt tools before their companies do, lawyers speak of negligence for not using AI, and a study warns that doing homework with AI reduces learning by 20%. This edition connects markets, work, regulation, and education to offer a practical reading.

Markets and Infrastructure: Money Moves in AI

Alibaba and SoftBank have mobilized more than €14.1 billion to accelerate their bet on artificial intelligence, according to EL PAÍS. This investment adds to the growing demand for connectivity and computing capacity driving the expansion of AI models, as detailed by TradingView. For investors, the message is clear: technological infrastructure (data centers, networks) is consolidating as a medium-term value opportunity.

In parallel, Telefónica describes how AI agents are transforming companies and administrations, reinforcing the need to invest in systems capable of executing autonomous tasks.

Work: The Gap Between Employees and Companies

Forbes Spain reveals that AI already drives three out of ten business tasks, widening the productivity gap between organizations. However, La Ecuación Digital warns of a mismatch: AI advances faster in workforces than in companies themselves, with employees adopting tools on their own before companies formalize policies. This situation generates security and governance risks, but also an opportunity for HR leaders to channel that momentum.

For small businesses and executives, the key is to align spontaneous adoption with corporate strategy: define permitted uses, train teams, and measure productivity impact. Failing to do so can widen the competitive gap that Forbes already points out.

Regulation and Professions: Training and Auditing to Stay Ahead

The legal sector issues a stark warning: according to El Español, lawyers agree that a professional can incur negligence by not using artificial intelligence. At the same time, Google brings Gemini to law firms with specialized agents, according to DiarioBitcoin, facilitating the automation of legal tasks. But adoption is not automatic: it requires judgment and oversight.

In healthcare, radiology demands training and audits to integrate AI into clinical practice, according to Univadis. This reflects a common pattern in regulated professions: technology promises efficiency, but without governance and training it can generate legal and clinical risks. For lawyers, doctors, and other professionals, the recommendation is to combine tools like Gemini with human review protocols.

Education: Redesigning Homework to Avoid Learning Loss

A study cited by La Vanguardia indicates that using AI to do homework and assignments reduces learning by 20%. At the same time, EL PAÍS proposes changing school homework in the age of AI to foster skills that technology cannot replicate, such as critical thinking and creativity. There is no contradiction: both sources agree that unsupervised use of AI in academic tasks harms knowledge retention.

For educators and parents, the practical utility is clear: instead of banning, design activities that integrate AI as a support tool, not a substitute. For example, ask students to compare their response with that of a model and argue the differences. This leverages technology without sacrificing deep learning.

What matters

  1. Investors should watch AI infrastructure (data centers, networks) following Alibaba and SoftBank's investment.
  2. Companies need to formalize AI policies to avoid losing the productivity advantage employees already gain.
  3. Regulated professionals (lawyers, doctors) must adopt AI with training and audits to avoid negligence and clinical risks.
  4. In education, banning AI is not the solution; homework must be redesigned to foster human skills and avoid learning loss.
  5. Adopting AI agents in administrations and companies requires governance to maximize efficiency without risks.

The day leaves a cross-cutting lesson: AI accelerates on all fronts, but its real value depends on governance, training, and process redesign. Investing in infrastructure or adopting agents without preparing people or auditing results widens existing gaps. The opportunity lies with those who integrate technology with ethical and operational judgment, from the medical consultation to the classroom.
